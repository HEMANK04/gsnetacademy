Introduction to Fallacies — तर्कदोष का परिचय

Fallacy reasoning में एक error है जो argument को invalid, weak, या unconvincing बनाता है।

Fallacy = Error in Reasoning + Makes Argument Weak/Invalid

Two Main Types:

  • Formal Fallacies — रूपगत तर्कदोष: Errors in the logical structure of a deductive argument.
  • Informal Fallacies — अनौपचारिक तर्कदोष: Errors in content, language, or context rather than structure.
2

Formal Fallacies — रूपगत तर्कदोष

Formal fallacies occur when there is a structural flaw in a deductive argument — the conclusion does not logically follow from the premises.

  • Undistributed Middle
  • Illicit Major
  • Illicit Minor
  • Exclusive Premises
  • Negative/Affirmative Conclusion Errors
  • Two Particular Premises
3

Undistributed Middle — अवितरित मध्य पद

The middle term (M) is not distributed in either premise.

Undistributed Middle = M is not distributed in any premise

Example:

  • All dogs (M) are animals (P).
  • All cats (S) are animals (M).
  • Therefore, all cats (S) are dogs (P).

Analysis: M is the predicate in both premises → not distributed in either → invalid.

4

Illicit Major — अवैध प्रमुख पद

The Major Term (P) is distributed in the conclusion but not distributed in the major premise.

Illicit Major = P distributed in conclusion but not in major premise

Example:

  • All cats are animals. (Major Premise — P=animals, not distributed)
  • No dogs are cats. (Minor Premise)
  • Therefore, no dogs are animals. (Conclusion — P=animals, distributed)
5

Illicit Minor — अवैध गौण पद

The Minor Term (S) is distributed in the conclusion but not distributed in the minor premise.

Illicit Minor = S distributed in conclusion but not in minor premise

Example:

  • All cats are animals. (Major Premise)
  • All cats are mammals. (Minor Premise — S=cats, distributed)
  • Therefore, all mammals are animals. (Conclusion — S=mammals, distributed but not in minor premise)
6

Exclusive Premises — दो नकारात्मक आधार

Both premises are negative (E or O).

Exclusive Premises = Two Negative Premises → Invalid

Example:

  • No cats are dogs. (E)
  • No animals are cats. (E)
  • Therefore, no animals are dogs. (Invalid)
7

Negative/Affirmative Conclusion Errors — नकारात्मक/सकारात्मक निष्कर्ष त्रुटि

  • If either premise is negative, conclusion must be negative.
  • If both premises are affirmative, conclusion must be affirmative.

Example (Negative premise → Affirmative conclusion):

  • No cats are dogs. (Negative)
  • All animals are cats. (Affirmative)
  • Therefore, all animals are dogs. (Affirmative — ❌ Invalid)
8

Two Particular Premises — दो विशेष आधार

Both premises are particular (I or O).

Two Particular Premises = No Conclusion → Invalid

Example:

  • Some cats are animals. (I)
  • Some dogs are cats. (I)
  • Therefore, some dogs are animals. (Invalid)
9

Conditional Fallacies — सशर्त तर्कदोष

Conditional fallacies occur in arguments with if...then (conditional) statements.

  • Affirming the Consequent
  • Denying the Antecedent
10

Affirming Consequent — परिणामी की पुष्टि

If P → Q; Q is true; Therefore P is true. (Invalid)

Structure:

  • If it rains (P), then the ground is wet (Q).
  • The ground is wet (Q).
  • Therefore, it rained (P).

Analysis: The ground could be wet for other reasons (sprinkler, etc.). This is invalid.

11

Denying Antecedent — पूर्वपद का निषेध

If P → Q; P is false; Therefore Q is false. (Invalid)

Structure:

  • If it rains (P), then the ground is wet (Q).
  • It does not rain (P is false).
  • Therefore, the ground is not wet (Q is false).

Analysis: The ground could still be wet (sprinkler, etc.). This is invalid.

12

Informal Fallacies — अनौपचारिक तर्कदोष

Informal fallacies occur due to content, language, ambiguity, or emotional manipulation rather than logical structure.

  • Relevance Fallacies — Ad Hominem, Appeal to Authority, Ad Populum, Ad Misericordiam, Ad Baculum, Ad Ignorantiam, Straw Man, Red Herring
  • Inductive Fallacies — Hasty Generalisation, False Cause/Post Hoc, Slippery Slope
  • Presumption Fallacies — False Dilemma, Begging the Question, Circular Reasoning
  • Ambiguity Fallacies — Equivocation, Amphiboly, Composition, Division
13

Ad Hominem — व्यक्ति-आघात

Attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself.

Ad Hominem = Attacking the Person, Not the Argument

Example:

  • "You can't trust his argument about climate change because he drives a big car."
  • Whether he drives a big car is irrelevant to the truth of his argument.
14

Appeal to Authority — अधिकार का आधार

Appealing to an authority figure who is not an expert in the relevant field.

Appeal to Authority = Using an irrelevant authority to support a claim

Example:

  • "This medicine must be good because a famous actor endorsed it."
  • An actor is not a medical expert.
15

Ad Populum — लोकप्रियता का आधार

Claiming something is true because many people believe it or it is popular.

Ad Populum = Popularity ≠ Truth

Example:

  • "Everyone knows that the earth is flat, so it must be true."
  • Popular belief does not make something true.
16

Ad Misericordiam — दया का आधार

Appealing to pity or sympathy to support a conclusion.

Ad Misericordiam = Appeal to Pity

Example:

  • "You should give me the job because I have a family to support."
  • While it may be sad, it does not prove you are qualified.
17

Ad Baculum — बल/धमकी का आधार

Using threats or fear to make someone accept a conclusion.

Ad Baculum = Appeal to Force/Threat

Example:

  • "If you don't agree with me, I will make your life difficult."
  • This does not make the argument true.
18

Ad Ignorantiam — अज्ञानता का आधार

Claiming something is true because it hasn't been proven false, or false because it hasn't been proven true.

Ad Ignorantiam = Appeal to Ignorance

Example:

  • "No one has proven that ghosts don't exist, so they must exist."
  • Lack of evidence against something does not prove it.
19

Straw Man — पुआल आदमी

Misrepresenting or oversimplifying someone's argument to make it easier to attack.

Straw Man = Distorting the Argument

Example:

  • Person A: "We should invest more in education."
  • Person B: "So you want to ignore healthcare and infrastructure?"
  • Person B misrepresents Person A's argument.
20

Red Herring — ध्यान-भटकाव

Introducing irrelevant information to distract from the main issue.

Red Herring = Changing the Subject

Example:

  • When asked about rising crime rates, a politician starts talking about the economy.
  • This is irrelevant to the crime rate question.
21

Hasty Generalisation — अतिशीघ्र सामान्यीकरण

Drawing a general conclusion from a small or unrepresentative sample.

Hasty Generalisation = Insufficient Sample → General Conclusion

Example:

  • "I met two rude people from that city, so everyone there is rude."
  • Two people are not enough to represent a whole city.
22

False Cause / Post Hoc — कारण-भ्रम / पश्चात-अतः

Assuming that because one event happened after another, the first event caused the second.

Post Hoc = X happened before Y → X caused Y (Invalid)

Example:

  • "I wore my lucky shirt and we won the game, so the shirt caused the win."
  • Correlation/timing does not equal causation.
23

Slippery Slope — फिसलन-ढाल

Arguing that one action will inevitably lead to a chain of negative events without evidence.

Slippery Slope = One Step → Inevitable Negative Chain

Example:

  • "If we allow students to use calculators, soon they will forget how to do basic math, and eventually they won't be able to think at all."
  • This is an unsupported chain of events.
24

False Dilemma — कूट दुविधा

Presenting only two options when there are actually more.

False Dilemma = Either X or Y (But more options exist)

Example:

  • "You are either with us or against us."
  • There are many possible positions in between.
25

Begging the Question — प्रश्न-भीख

Assuming what you are trying to prove. The conclusion is already assumed in the premises.

Begging the Question = Conclusion assumed in premises

Example:

  • "The book is true because it says it is true."
  • The conclusion is assumed in the premise.
26

Circular Reasoning — वृत्ताकार तर्क

Similar to begging the question — the conclusion is used to support the premises in a circle.

Circular Reasoning = Premise A depends on Conclusion B, Conclusion B depends on Premise A

Example:

  • "I am trustworthy because I always tell the truth. I always tell the truth because I am trustworthy."
  • This reasoning goes in a circle.
27

Equivocation — समशब्द-भ्रम

Using the same word in different meanings within an argument.

Equivocation = Same Word, Different Meanings

Example:

  • "A feather is light. What is light cannot be dark. Therefore, a feather cannot be dark."
  • "Light" means weight in the first use and brightness in the second.
28

Amphiboly — अस्पष्ट वाक्य-भ्रम

Ambiguous sentence structure leading to multiple interpretations.

Amphiboly = Grammatical Ambiguity

Example:

  • "I saw the man with the telescope."
  • Did I use the telescope to see the man, or did the man have the telescope?
29

Composition — संयोजन-भ्रम

Assuming that what is true of the parts is also true of the whole.

Composition = Parts → Whole (Invalid)

Example:

  • "Each player on the team is excellent, so the team must be excellent."
  • Individual excellence does not guarantee team success.
30

Division — विभाजन-भ्रम

Assuming that what is true of the whole is also true of the parts.

Division = Whole → Parts (Invalid)

Example:

  • "The team is the best in the league, so every player on the team must be the best in the league."
  • A team can be great without every player being individually great.
31

Common Exam Traps — सामान्य परीक्षा जाल

  • Trap 1: Ad Hominem = only personal insults → ❌ It includes attacking character, motives, or circumstances.
  • Trap 2: Appeal to Authority = always fallacious → ❌ It's only fallacious when the authority is not relevant or credible.
  • Trap 3: Ad Populum = majority rule → ❌ Popularity does not prove truth.
  • Trap 4: Post Hoc = causation → ❌ Temporal sequence does not prove causation.
  • Trap 5: Slippery Slope = always fallacious → ❌ It is fallacious only when the chain of events is unsupported.
  • Trap 6: False Dilemma = only two options → ❌ Usually more than two options exist.
  • Trap 7: Equivocation = same word → ❌ Same word with different meanings.
32

Practice Questions — अभ्यास प्रश्न

Question 1

"You can't trust his argument about climate change because he is not a scientist." This is an example of:

  • A. Ad Hominem
  • B. Appeal to Authority
  • C. Straw Man
  • D. Ad Populum
Answer: A. Ad Hominem

Explanation: Attacking the person (he is not a scientist) instead of addressing the argument itself.

Question 2

"Everyone is buying this product, so it must be good." This is an example of:

  • A. Ad Hominem
  • B. Ad Populum
  • C. False Dilemma
  • D. Begging the Question
Answer: B. Ad Populum

Explanation: Appealing to popularity to prove quality — Ad Populum.

Question 3

All cats are animals. All dogs are animals. Therefore, all dogs are cats. This commits which fallacy?

  • A. Undistributed Middle
  • B. Illicit Major
  • C. Exclusive Premises
  • D. Affirming Consequent
Answer: A. Undistributed Middle

Explanation: Middle term "animals" is not distributed in either premise.

Question 4

"If you don't support this policy, you are unpatriotic." This is an example of:

  • A. False Dilemma
  • B. Ad Baculum
  • C. Red Herring
  • D. Slippery Slope
Answer: A. False Dilemma

Explanation: Presents only two options — support policy or be unpatriotic — ignoring other possibilities.

Question 5

"I wore my lucky socks and we won the game, so the socks caused the win." This is an example of:

  • A. Hasty Generalisation
  • B. False Cause/Post Hoc
  • C. Slippery Slope
  • D. Equivocation
Answer: B. False Cause/Post Hoc

Explanation: Assuming causation because one event happened after another.

One-Page Revision — एक पेज में पुनरावृत्ति

Fallacy = Error in Reasoning

Formal Fallacies:

  • Undistributed Middle, Illicit Major, Illicit Minor, Exclusive Premises, Negative/Affirmative Conclusion Errors, Two Particular Premises

Conditional Fallacies:

  • Affirming Consequent, Denying Antecedent

Informal Fallacies:

  • Relevance: Ad Hominem, Appeal to Authority, Ad Populum, Ad Misericordiam, Ad Baculum, Ad Ignorantiam, Straw Man, Red Herring
  • Inductive: Hasty Generalisation, False Cause/Post Hoc, Slippery Slope
  • Presumption: False Dilemma, Begging the Question, Circular Reasoning
  • Ambiguity: Equivocation, Amphiboly, Composition, Division

Exam Formula:

Identify Fallacy → Name It → Explain Why It's Fallacious
